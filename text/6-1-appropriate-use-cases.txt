

## Identifying Appropriate and Inappropriate Use Cases
Learn this interactivelyConcept Check|Build Coach

## What You Need to Know
Two questions decide whether a task goes to Claude. Fit asks whether Claude would do the work well: the right shape, the right volume, output someone can check. Domain 4 covers fit. Appropriateness asks whether the task should be delegated to Claude at all. It looks at different evidence: the stakes, the authority the work requires, and who is accountable for the outcome. A task can be a perfect practical fit and still be work that only a qualified human should do.

## Fit Is Not Permission
This lesson uses one made-up example throughout: a bike shop. Its owner has two jobs to do. One is a long series of customer reviews to summarise into themes. The other is a stack of applications for a mechanic's job, to score so that the owner can pick the person to hire. Scoring the applications is an excellent practical fit: structured, voluminous, checkable. It can still be inappropriate, because of what the scoring decides and who cannot challenge it. The wrong answer that appears often bases its reasoning on quality: Claude would do the task quickly and well, so it must be fine to delegate. The quality of the output does not decide whether the task is appropriate.

## The Four Inappropriate Categories
Four recognisable categories make a use inappropriate.

The first is work that needs professional authority: legal, medical, financial and safeguarding judgements. A qualified person must make these and be accountable for them. Claude can help someone prepare, summarise or understand. Claude cannot be the source of the advice. At the bike shop, medical advice for an injured customer must come from a qualified person. The Usage Policy names the settings where this category applies most. Its High-risk Areas include legal and medical advice, finance, credit, insurance, housing, employment, education and healthcare access. In these areas a qualified person must review Claude's recommendation before it is given as advice or used to make a decision. The policy also states: "The qualified person remains responsible for the accuracy and appropriateness of what is delivered or decided." The policy excludes wellness content, such as education on sleep, stress, nutrition or exercise.

The second is a consequential decision about identifiable people: who gets the job, the award, the sanction, the visit. Poor output is not the reason. Nobody is accountable for the decision, and the person affected cannot challenge it. The scoring of the mechanic applications belongs in this category.

The third is fabrication presented as real: invented quotes, testimonials, case studies or evidence, such as customer testimonials the shop writes itself. Plausibility makes fabrication worse, because a convincing invention is easier to believe. The Usage Policy's prohibitions on misinformation and deceptive practices are the basis of this category.

The fourth is anything the Usage Policy or the organisation's own policy prohibits. Objective 6.3 covers this category.

## The Substitution Test
One test decides whether a task is appropriate. Picture a capable, well-meaning colleague who has:

no professional qualification in the area

no access to the person's history

no accountability for the outcome

Would you accept this piece of work from them? The test changes an abstract question about appropriateness into an ordinary judgement about delegation. Every professional already makes that judgement. The review summary passes: the same colleague could write it, and the owner would check it. The mechanic scoring fails on all three grounds at once. Anthropic's AI Fluency framework names this skill Delegation.

## Most Work Is Appropriate
The broad range of appropriate uses matters as much as the four categories. Official documentation describes a deliberately broad range of appropriate uses: writing across formats, summarising long documents, brainstorming, analysing images and data, translating. Drafting, reformatting, analysing material you hold, and preparing someone to make a decision are all appropriate. The inappropriate categories are narrow and specific. If you treat everything near them as forbidden, you abandon work that a simple safeguard would enable. The owner can still ask Claude to summarise each application, then read the applications and choose.

## In the exam
This objective asks a different question from the rest of the certification. Candidates who come to it from Domain 4 reliably answer the wrong question. The substitution test covers everything the exam asks. The exam also marks over-caution as wrong: the right answer almost always makes the work possible within the rules.
What to notice in the exam
Adding review to an inappropriate delegation does not repair it. Suppose a manager checks the output of a consequential decision about identifiable people. The check restores neither accountability for the judgement nor the affected person's ability to challenge it. When the owner reads every applicant's score, neither of those two things changes. Exam options that offer review as the fix test exactly this point.

The Usage Policy's review rule answers a different question. It applies where a use in a High-risk Area proceeds. For that case, the policy states this requirement: "A qualified person must meaningfully review a High-risk AI Recommendation, with the authority to change or modify it, before it is delivered as advice". There, review is a mandatory control, and objective 2.4 teaches it as the standard. Review cannot convert a decision that should not have been delegated into one that should. Ask whether the delegation is appropriate first. Only then does the review requirement apply.

The AI Fluency framework lists the four Ds as module names and does not define them. Reading Delegation as "deciding what to hand to AI and what to keep" is our own interpretation. The Help Center's use-case article lists what Claude is for and contains no prohibitions. The inappropriate categories are based on the Usage Policy and the exam guide.

## Exam Traps

Exam Trap
Adding a human reviewer to an inappropriate delegation and treating that as the fix
Adding review to an inappropriate delegation does not repair it. A reviewer who approves a decision made elsewhere restores neither the judgement nor a reasoned answer to the affected person's appeal. Keep the decision with an accountable person.

Exam Trap
Accepting the argument that a use is fine because the results stay inside the organisation
Keeping an output in-house restores neither accountability for the decision it contains nor the affected person's ability to challenge it. Keep the decision with a named, accountable person.

Exam Trap
Declining both halves of a mixed proposal
Declining everything looks cautious but loses help Claude can appropriately give. When a proposal mixes appropriate and inappropriate work, keep the appropriate half and decline only the inappropriate one.

## Practice Scenario
A children's centre manager is asked to use Claude on the annual family survey. Two proposals arrive together: summarise three hundred free-text responses into themes for the service review, and score individual families' responses to flag which should receive a home visit. The service review needs the themes. Centre policy allows Claude on the survey text and bars any tool from scoring families for home visits. What should the manager do?
Option ASummarise the responses into themes once identifying details are removed, and keep the home-visit flagging with the qualified staff of the centre
Option BRun the scoring as proposed, and have the deputy manager review every flagged family before any visit, so a person stays involved
Option CDecline both proposals, since family survey material is too sensitive for any AI tool to touch
Option DRun both pieces of work, since the survey data never leaves the centre and the families consented to its use for service planning when they completed the survey
