

## Adjusting Your Approach Based on Feedback and Results
Learn this interactivelyConcept Check|Build Coach

## What You Need to Know
An adjustment round means changing a request, running it again and judging the output. Objective 1.3 taught what good feedback sounds like inside a conversation. This objective covers the discipline around that feedback. A systematic round gives you more than a different result. It gives you knowledge that you can reuse tomorrow and explain to a colleague. A systematic round needs three parts. You change one thing at a time. You hold the test input fixed. You judge the result against a criterion that you state before you look.

This lesson uses one made-up example throughout: a carpet shop. It has Claude draft the quote letter that the shop sends after each measuring visit. Customers continue to phone the shop to ask for the total.

## Change One Thing at a Time
One change at a time shows what fixed the output. Suppose the owner changes only the audience line and the letter improves. Then the audience line was the problem, and that lesson applies to every similar task. Suppose she changes the wording, the model and the attached example at once. The letter improves, and nobody knows which of the three worked. Tomorrow, when she does the same kind of task, she knows no more than before. A bundled change, which changes several things at once, looks efficient until you need a second good result. Anthropic's Help Center says that an effective prompt contains as much context as possible. About adjustment, the Help Center gives no advice beyond giving Claude feedback, clarifications and rewrite requests. The one-variable discipline, one change at a time, is ordinary professional method.

## Hold the Test Input Fixed
A new prompt that you try on a new document tells you nothing, because two things changed. A better result may only mean an easier input. When you hold the test input fixed (the same course, report or source file), the comparison between rounds is meaningful. For the owner, the test input is one customer's measuring notes. She uses these notes in every round. When you edit an earlier message, the edit creates a different version of the chat. That version has its own artifacts. Each round can start from the same input, and you can compare its result with the round before.

## State the Criterion Before You Look
"Better" is not a criterion. "A customer can find the total price in five seconds" is a criterion. Stating the criterion in advance stops your judgement from drifting towards whatever the new draft happens to do well. That drift is how you accept a round that fixed nothing. A stated criterion also makes other people's reactions usable. A returned draft and a criterion together identify which requirement failed. "The manager didn't like it" identifies nothing.

## Feedback Arrives from Readers, Not Just the Text
The results that matter often come from outside the conversation. A colleague says the tone is wrong. A manager returns a draft. A reader asks the same clarifying question three times. These reactions usually show what the output lacks more precisely than you do when you re-read the text yourself. The customers who phone about the total are such a result. When you treat these results as inputs to the next round, not as noise, you can iterate on whether the output works. When you ignore them, you iterate on how the output looks.

## Know When to Stop Adjusting
Adjustment assumes that the approach is right and that the execution is wrong. When several well-aimed rounds have not changed the output, that assumption has failed. The task may need different source material, a feature such as web search or Research, or a different setting. The task may be work that you should not give to Claude at all. The third failed adjustment is a signal to return to the cause list in 7.1 and diagnose the cause again. A fourth round of slightly different words tests the same failed assumption. If customers still phone after the owner's third round, she stops rewording.

## In the exam
The exam has questions on each of the three parts. The exam presents the bundled change as decisiveness.
Where this method comes from
No Anthropic article teaches one-variable adjustment. The Help Center's advice ends at giving Claude feedback and asking for rewrites. Branching a chat only gives you versions to compare. This lesson's discipline is based on the wording of the exam guide's objective and on ordinary professional practice.

## Exam Traps

Exam Trap
Changing several things at once and calling it decisive
Changing the wording, the model and the example in one round leaves nobody knowing which change worked, so the improvement cannot be attributed or repeated. Change one thing at a time and keep the rest fixed.

Exam Trap
A fourth round of rewording after three aimed rounds changed nothing
Several well-aimed rounds that changed nothing mean the approach, not the execution, is wrong. Go back to 7.1's cause list and re-diagnose: different source material, a different feature or setting, or work not to be handed over.

Exam Trap
Testing the adjusted prompt on a different document and crediting the prompt
When the input changed too, the comparison is meaningless; the improvement may only mean the new document was easier. Round-to-round learning requires the same test input every time.

## Practice Scenario
The owner of a ceramics studio drafts the joining email she sends attendees before each weekend workshop, and attendees keep saying it is too long and too formal. She has time for one adjustment round this week. Which round is set up to teach her something?
Option AAdd one line naming the reader, re-run the same workshop’s email, and judge it against a criterion set in advance: an attendee can find the start time in five seconds
Option BAsk Claude to make the email warmer and shorter, and adopt the new version if it reads better
Option CRun the unchanged prompt on a different workshop’s joining email to see whether the problem follows the prompt or that particular workshop
Option DRewrite the prompt, switch to a different model and attach a friendlier example email all in the same round, then keep whichever combination works best
